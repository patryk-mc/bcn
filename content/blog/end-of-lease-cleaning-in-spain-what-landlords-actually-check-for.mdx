End-of-Lease Cleaning in Spain: What Landlords Actually Check For

*By Lyn, Founder of BCN Ideal Services*

---

Every few months, I get some version of the same message from a client: "I did everything right, and I still lost part of my deposit." Usually, it's not because they did something wrong; it's because nobody told them what "doing it right" actually means under Spanish rental law.

After years of helping expats settle into (and move out of) homes across Barcelona, I've learned that most deposit disputes aren't really about cleanliness at all. They're about documentation, timing, and knowing what a landlord is legally allowed to deduct versus what they're just hoping you won't question. So I wanted to put together the guide I wish someone had handed me when I first moved here.

## How Rental Deposits Actually Work in Spain

Let's start with the basics, because most of the confusion begins here.

Under Article 36 of Spain's *Ley de Arrendamientos Urbanos* (LAU), every residential lease must include a cash deposit, the *fianza*, equal to exactly one month's rent. That money is meant to be held for the duration of the tenancy and returned at the end of the lease, minus any deductions the landlord can justify ([Waypoint Sur](https://guides.waypointsur.com/renting-in-spain/)).

Here's the part a lot of people don't realize: landlords can ask for more than just the fianza, but there's a hard ceiling on it. On top of the one-month deposit, they're allowed to request an additional guarantee, a second deposit, a bank guarantee, or a personal guarantor, capped at two more months' rent. Add it up, and the absolute legal maximum anyone should be asked to pay upfront is three months' rent total. If a landlord is asking for more than that, they're not following the law ([Expat Helper](https://expathelper.es/long-term-rental-contracts-spain-guide/)).

In Catalonia specifically, that deposit is meant to be registered with the regional housing authority, INCASOL, rather than simply kept by the landlord ([Rental Contract Spain Guide](https://guides.waypointsur.com/rental-contract-spain/)). Worth knowing, because deposit protection rules aren't identical everywhere in Spain, some regions have recently scaled theirs back, which only makes good documentation on your end more important, not less.

## The 30-Day Rule Almost Nobody Mentions

If there's one number to remember from this entire post, it's thirty.

Once your lease ends and you've handed back the keys, your landlord has one month to return your deposit. Not "eventually." Not "whenever it's convenient." One month, full stop, and if they go past that window, they legally owe you interest on the amount they're holding ([Tenant Rights in Spain](https://guides.waypointsur.com/tenant-rights-spain/)).

I mention this because I've seen too many expats accept a vague "we'll get to it" from a landlord, not realizing there's an actual legal clock running the moment they leave.

## "Clean" Isn't the Same as "As You Found It." And That's the Real Issue

This is where most disputes actually live.

Deductions from your deposit are only valid when a landlord can back them up, with photos, invoices, or a professional assessment showing genuine damage. And critically, ordinary wear and tear doesn't count. A scuff on a well-used floor, slightly faded paint, normal signs that a home was lived in, none of that is a valid reason to withhold part of your deposit, because Spanish law explicitly recognizes that everyday use of a property is expected, not a violation of your lease ([How to Get Your Rental Deposit Back in Spain](https://expathelper.es/rental-deposit-spain-guide/)).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much is a rental deposit (fianza) in Spain?**\
Under Article 36 of the LAU, the legal deposit for a residential lease is exactly one month’s rent. Landlords may request an additional guarantee, capped at two more months’ rent, bringing the legal maximum you should ever be asked for upfront to three months’ rent total.

**How long does a landlord have to return my deposit?**\
One month from the day your lease ends and you hand back the keys. If they go past that window, they legally owe you interest on the amount they’re still holding.

**Can my landlord deduct money for normal wear and tear?**\
No. Spanish law explicitly recognizes that everyday use of a property- a bit of fading, minor scuffs, normal signs of living there- is expected, not damage. Any deduction must be supported by real evidence: photos, invoices, or a professional assessment of genuine damage.

The problem is, a lot of landlords lean on vague language, "not returned in the same condition,"  without ever specifying what that actually means. That's exactly why documentation matters so much on your end.

## The One Habit That Prevents Almost Every Dispute

If I could get every client to do just one thing before they move out, it would be this: **photograph everything, both at move-in and move-out.**

The strongest contracts include a signed inventory annex, a documented record of the property's condition, furniture, and appliances at the start of the tenancy. If your contract doesn't include one, don't assume that protects you; it does the opposite. Create your own. Photos, dated, ideally sent to your landlord in writing so there's a timestamp on record, and signed by both parties if you can manage it ([Renting in Spain Guide](https://guides.waypointsur.com/renting-in-spain/)).

I know it feels excessive when you're already juggling a move. But that folder of photos is often the single difference between a smooth deposit return and a month of frustrating back-and-forth.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

Here's the short version of everything above, the way I'd explain it to a client sitting across from me:

- Your legal deposit is one month's rent, no more, unless it's a clearly documented additional guarantee, capped at two extra months.
- Your landlord has 30 days after you hand back the keys to return it.
- They can only deduct for real, evidenced damage, not normal wear and tear.
- Photos and a signed inventory, at both move-in and move-out, are your best protection.
- If your deposit was meant to be registered with a regional authority like INCASOL and wasn't, that's worth asking about too.
